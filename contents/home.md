#### About
##### Research Interests

I investigate how human language structures meaning. I also have interests in understanding the inductive biases that explain how we come to acquire the rules by which language structures meaning, and in how we process and comprehend sentences. I make use of a variety of methodological approaches, and accordingly, I'm equally comfortable calling myself a syntactician and semanticist, a computational linguist, and a psycholinguist.

I'm particularly interested in questions relating to verbal argument structure: What rules govern how verbs assign meanings to their dependents, and how does this depend on the positions and syntactic categories of those dependents? Are the inductive biases and learning strategies that enable us to know these rules innate and language-specific, or are they learnable from linguistic input? How do the structures and meanings these rules create affect language processing? What are the universals in this domain, what varies, and what is the best way to model these?

Much of my work has focused on alternations: relationships between clauses that differ in how a verb assigns meanings to its dependents, taken broadly. In recent work, I've investigated how people and language models generalize the use of the active-passive alternation to novel verbs. I've also investigated the syntax and semantics of the _spray_/_load_ alternation and resultatives, and found deep similarities between them that indicate they each involve syntactic argument sharing. My recent psycholinguistic work is focused on understanding how the way in which two types of verbs that assign experiencer roles to one of their arguments affects sentence processing and comprehension.

My overall goal in my research is to develop our understanding of the relationship between syntax and semantics, in particular in the domain of verbal argument structure. In doing so, my goal is to develop our understanding of the human language faculty and how it interfaces with cognition more broadly. ##### Academic history

I'm an assistant professor of computational linguistics at the University of Nevada, Reno, a post I have held since 2025. I am also an affiliated assistant professor at the University of Delaware, in which capacity I've advised several graduate students. Before I came to UNR, I was a visiting assistant professor at UD, and before that, I was a post-doc in Bob Frank's computational linguistics lab at Yale. I completed my PhD at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, where my advisors were Kyle Johnson, Rajesh Bhatt, and Seth Cable. Before that, I majored in linguistics and Spanish at the University of Texas, Austin (where I grew up).
